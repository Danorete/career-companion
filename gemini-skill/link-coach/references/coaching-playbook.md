# Coaching playbook

Use this when a student needs to prepare for, have, or follow up on a conversation. Keep answers
short. Offer the next small step.

## I don't know where to start (no contacts yet)

Start with people they already have. Ask: "Who are 3 people you know a little? A classmate, a
professor, a club leader, someone from a job or an event?" Then pick one and help with a
first message. Reasons to reach out that need no special access:

- A class project, club, or event you both took part in
- A talk, article, or project they shared
- A question only they can answer, asked in one line
- A small offer, such as sending a useful link

Do not suggest messaging strangers in bulk. One real message beats ten generic ones.

## Before a conversation (coffee chat, career fair, meetup)

Give a short plan, not an essay:

1. **Why**: one sentence on why you are meeting them
2. **Two questions**: specific to their work or path (see below)
3. **One thing to offer**: a project update, a link, or a thank-you for their time
4. **How you will remember**: decide now where you will write 3 lines afterward

Good questions (pick two that fit):

- "How did you move into [their field]?"
- "What did you not know when you started that you wish you had?"
- "What would you look for in someone starting out?"
- "Is there something you read or did that helped you most?"
- "Who else would you suggest I learn from?" (ask only after a good conversation)

Offer to practise: "Want to try the first 30 seconds out loud with me?"

## During

- Listen for a reason: something they say you can connect to later.
- It is fine to write a quick note in the moment ("May I jot that down?").
- If you freeze, say: "That's interesting. Can you tell me more about that?"
- Ask for contact info or a LinkedIn connection before you leave. Many students forget this.

## After (the first 48 hours matter)

1. Write 3 lines: where we met, what we talked about, what I promised.
2. Send a short follow-up within 1 to 2 days (use the four-part pattern).
3. Do the thing you promised first. Keeping a small promise builds trust faster than a long message.
4. If they don't reply, wait about 2 weeks, then send one short update with something new.
   Stop after two tries. No reply is information, not an insult.

## When students say...

- "I don't know what to say." Offer a draft with brackets, then ask for the missing detail.
- "They won't want to hear from me." Remind them one short, polite message costs the other person
  about 20 seconds, and give a no-pressure line.
- "I'm too busy." Offer the 10-minute version: one line, one detail, send.
- "I forgot to get their info." Look for a mutual contact, the event organizer, or a class or club list.
  Never guess an email address.
